## Lesson 3.1: Why Budgets Fail (And How Yours Won't)

### Introduction

Let's get this out of the way: **budgets have a terrible reputation.** They feel like diets for your money—restrictive, joyless, and doomed to fail by the second week.

But here's the thing: most budgets don't fail because *you* failed. They fail because they were set up to fail from the start.

In this lesson, we're going to look at the most common reasons budgets crash and burn, and then we'll build a completely different approach—one designed for real life, real slip-ups, and real people who just went through bankruptcy.

> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "I used to think budgets were about telling myself 'no' all the time. Turns out, a good budget is really about telling yourself 'yes'—to the things that actually matter."
>
> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "Same! I thought budgeting meant I couldn't have fun anymore. But it's more like a GPS. It doesn't tell you where to go—it just helps you get where *you* want to go."

---

### The 5 Reasons Most Budgets Fail

#### 1. The "Too Tight" Budget

**What it looks like:** You slash everything to the bone. No eating out. No streaming. No coffee. Zero fun money.

**Why it fails:** It's the financial equivalent of a crash diet. You might last a week, maybe two. Then you "break" and spend $200 on things you didn't even want, just because you felt deprived.

**The fix:** Build in guilt-free spending. Seriously. A budget that includes $50 for "whatever I want" will outperform a "perfect" budget every single time.

#### 2. The "Fantasy" Budget

**What it looks like:** You budget based on what you *wish* you spent, not what you *actually* spend. You write down $200/month for groceries when you've been spending $450.

**Why it fails:** By week two, the numbers don't match reality, so you throw the whole thing out.

**The fix:** Track your real spending for 2–4 weeks *before* you set budget amounts. We'll do this in Lesson 3.5.

#### 3. The "Set It and Forget It" Budget

**What it looks like:** You make a beautiful spreadsheet in January. You never look at it again.

**Why it fails:** Life changes. Expenses change. A budget that worked in January might not work in March.

**The fix:** Schedule a weekly 10-minute check-in and a monthly budget review. Put it on your calendar. Treat it like a doctor's appointment.

#### 4. The "No Tracking" Budget

**What it looks like:** You make a plan but never actually check whether you're following it. You just... hope.

**Why it fails:** Hope is not a financial strategy. Without tracking, you have no idea if you're on course until the money runs out.

**The fix:** Pick one tracking method and use it consistently. That could be an app, a notebook, an envelope system, or a spreadsheet. We'll explore the envelope system in Module 4.

#### 5. The "Shame Spiral" Budget

**What it looks like:** You overspend one category by $30 and think "I ruined it. I can't do this. Why even try?"

**Why it fails:** One slip-up doesn't ruin a budget any more than one bad meal ruins a healthy lifestyle. But shame makes you quit.

**The fix:** Expect imperfection. When you overspend, *adjust*—don't abandon. Move money from another category. Learn from it. Move on.

### The 4 Principles That Actually Work

Now that we know what *doesn't* work, here's what *does*:

#### Principle 1: Flexibility Over Perfection

A good budget bends. It doesn't break. Build in buffer categories. Use ranges instead of exact numbers when you're starting out. ("Groceries: $350–$450" is more realistic than "Groceries: $387.")

#### Principle 2: Track, Don't Judge

Tracking your spending isn't about catching yourself being "bad." It's about gathering data. Scientists don't get mad at their data. They use it. You're a scientist studying your own financial life.

#### Principle 3: Review Regularly

- **Weekly:** 10 minutes. Are you roughly on track? Any surprises?
- **Monthly:** 30 minutes. What worked? What didn't? What changes for next month?
- **Quarterly:** Did my income change? Did my goals change? Does my budget still reflect my life?

#### Principle 4: Compassion Is a Financial Strategy

This might sound like a motivational poster, but it's backed by research. People who treat slip-ups with self-compassion are *more* likely to get back on track than people who beat themselves up. Being kind to yourself isn't soft—it's strategic.

## Lesson 3.2: Calculating Your Real Income

**Type:** Interactive Exercise | **Time:** 40 min | **XP:** 100 (Lesson) + 50 (Quiz) + 50 (Reflection)
**Chapter-Specific Content**

### Opening Check-In

> Quick question: Do you know your exact monthly take-home pay? Not your salary—the amount that actually hits your bank account?
>
> If you said "not exactly," you're not alone. Most people don't. By the end of this lesson, you will.

---

### Introduction

Before you can build a budget, you need to answer one question: **How much money do I actually have to work with?**

This sounds simple, but it's trickier than you'd think—especially after bankruptcy. Your income situation may have changed. Your deductions might be different. If you have variable income, the number changes every month.

We're going to nail this down using two methods:

1. **The Average Method** — best if your income is fairly consistent
2. **The Baseline Method** — best if your income varies a lot

**Key term — Take-home pay (net income):** The amount deposited in your bank account after taxes, insurance, retirement contributions, and other deductions are removed. This is the *real* number we budget with. Not your salary. Not your gross pay. The money you can actually spend.

> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "I used to say I made $40,000 a year. But after taxes and deductions, I was actually bringing home about $2,600 a month. That's the number that matters for budgeting!"
>
> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "And if you get paid every two weeks, you get 26 paychecks a year—not 24. Two months a year, you get an 'extra' paycheck. That matters too."

---

### Method 1: The Average Method

**Best for:** People with steady paychecks (same employer, consistent hours).

**How it works:** Add up your last 12 months of take-home pay, then divide by 12.

**Step-by-step:**

| Month | Take-Home Pay |
|-------|--------------|
| Month 1 | $_______ |
| Month 2 | $_______ |
| Month 3 | $_______ |
| Month 4 | $_______ |
| Month 5 | $_______ |
| Month 6 | $_______ |
| Month 7 | $_______ |
| Month 8 | $_______ |
| Month 9 | $_______ |
| Month 10 | $_______ |
| Month 11 | $_______ |
| Month 12 | $_______ |
| **TOTAL** | **$_______** |
| **÷ 12 = Monthly Average** | **$_______** |

**Where to find this info:**
- Bank statements (deposits)
- Pay stubs
- Your employer's payroll portal
- Tax return (Line 1 of your 1040, but this is *gross*—use bank deposits for *net*)

**Don't have 12 months?** Use whatever you have. 6 months ÷ 6. 3 months ÷ 3. Even 1 month is a starting point.

---

> ### 💡 What If I Don't Have a Bank Account?
>
> If you're unbanked—using check-cashing services, receiving cash payments, or relying on prepaid cards—you can still calculate your income. Here's how:
>
> - **Check stubs or pay envelopes:** If your employer gives you a paper stub, save them. Add up the net amounts.
> - **Money order receipts:** If you buy money orders to pay bills, your receipts show how much cash you had to work with.
> - **Keep a simple income log:** Get a small notebook. Every time you receive money—paycheck, cash payment, side job—write the date, source, and amount. This becomes your bank statement.
> - **Estimate from receipts:** If you pay for everything in cash, save your receipts for 2–4 weeks and add them up. Your spending ≈ your income (if you're not saving or borrowing).
> - **Prepaid card statements:** Most prepaid cards have an app or website where you can see deposits and transactions. Use those like bank statements.
>
> **Looking ahead:** Module 6 covers opening a bank account after bankruptcy. Having one makes income tracking much easier—but you don't need one to start budgeting *right now*.

---

### Method 2: The Baseline Method

**Best for:** Freelancers, gig workers, commission-based earners, seasonal workers, or anyone whose income swings significantly month to month.

**How it works:** Look at your last 12 months of income and find the **lowest** month. That's your baseline—the amount you can *count on*.

**Step-by-step:**

1. List all 12 months of take-home pay (same table as above)
2. Circle the **lowest** month: $________
3. That's your **baseline budget number**

**Why the lowest?** Because if you can live on your worst month, every other month gives you extra to save, pay down debt, or invest. You're never caught short.

**What about the "extra" money in good months?**
Give it a job *before* you spend it:
- 50% → Emergency fund (until you hit your goal)
- 25% → Debt payoff or savings goal
- 25% → Reward yourself (you earned it!)

These percentages are suggestions—adjust to fit your situation.

## Lesson 3.3: The 50/30/20 Blueprint

**Type:** Instructional + Exercise | **Time:** 45 min | **XP:** 100 (Lesson) + 50 (Quiz) + 50 (Reflection)

### Introduction

There are dozens of budgeting methods out there. We're going to start with one of the simplest and most popular: the **50/30/20 rule.**

It was popularized by Senator Elizabeth Warren (back when she was a Harvard bankruptcy professor—fitting, right?) in her book *All Your Worth.* The idea is beautifully simple:

- **50% of take-home pay → Needs** (things you must pay to survive)
- **30% of take-home pay → Wants** (things that make life enjoyable)
- **20% of take-home pay → Savings & Debt Repayment** (future you)

That's it. Three categories. Three percentages. One framework.

> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "I love this method because it's simple enough to remember but flexible enough to actually work. You don't need to track 47 categories."
>
> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "And the 30% for wants isn't a luxury—it's what keeps you from burning out on your budget! It's built-in permission to enjoy your money."

---

### The Three Categories Explained

#### 50% — Needs

**Definition:** Expenses you *must* pay to maintain basic living. If you didn't pay these, there would be serious consequences (homelessness, no food, legal trouble, health crisis).

**Examples:**
- Rent or mortgage
- Utilities (electric, gas, water, basic phone)
- Groceries (not restaurants—basic food)
- Minimum debt payments (car loan, student loans, medical debt)
- Health insurance premiums
- Car payment + insurance (if needed for work)
- Child care (if needed for work)
- Essential medications

**The test:** "If I lost my job, would I still *have* to pay this?" If yes, it's probably a need.

#### 30% — Wants

**Definition:** Things that improve your quality of life but aren't required for survival. You could live without them, even if you wouldn't enjoy it.

**Examples:**
- Dining out, takeout, coffee shops
- Streaming services (Netflix, Spotify, etc.)
- Gym membership
- Hobbies and entertainment
- Shopping beyond basics (clothes you *want* vs. clothes you *need*)
- Vacations and travel
- Upgraded phone plan beyond basic
- Pet expenses beyond basics (grooming, toys, premium food)

**Important:** Wants aren't bad. Wants aren't wasteful. Wants are part of being human. This category is *supposed* to be here.

#### 20% — Savings & Debt Repayment

**Definition:** Money that goes toward building your financial future or paying off debt faster than required.

**Examples:**
- Emergency fund contributions
- Extra debt payments (above the minimum)
- Retirement savings
- Savings for specific goals (car, home, education)
- Investing

**Post-bankruptcy note:** If you're rebuilding from zero savings, this 20% is critical. Even $50/month into an emergency fund changes your financial resilience dramatically.

### "But My Needs Are More Than 50%!"

This is extremely common, especially after bankruptcy. Here's what to do:

**If needs = 50–60%:** This is normal for many people, especially in high-cost areas. Adjust to 60/20/20 or 55/25/20. The framework is a *guideline*, not a law.

**If needs = 60–70%:** You're stretched thin. Focus on the biggest needs (usually housing and transportation) and explore whether any can be reduced over time. Even 70/15/15 is a workable starting point.

**If needs > 70%:** Your income may not cover basic expenses. This is a signal to focus on increasing income (Module 7) or getting assistance. It's not a failure—it's information.

:::chapter-7
> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "Right after my Chapter 7, my needs were about 65% of my income. That's okay! I adjusted to 65/20/15 and worked on increasing my income over time. Within a year, I was closer to the 50/30/20 target. It's a direction, not a destination."
:::

:::chapter-13
> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "During my plan, my needs were basically 100% because the trustee payment ate everything. Now that I'm done? My needs dropped to about 55%. That freed-up money is going straight into the savings and wants categories. I'm adjusting gradually."
:::

## Lesson 3.4: Needs vs. Wants vs. Savings

**Type:** Interactive Sorting Exercise | **Time:** 30 min | **XP:** 100 (Lesson) + 50 (Quiz) + 50 (Reflection)
**Badge Available:** 🎯 Needs vs Wants Master (perfect sort)

### Opening Check-In

> True or false: You're confident you can tell the difference between a "need" and a "want."
>
> If you said "true"—great. We're about to test that. If you said "false"—perfect. That's what this lesson is for. Either way, some of these will surprise you.

---

### Introduction

In Lesson 3.3, we introduced needs, wants, and savings. Now we're going to sharpen that skill with a deep-dive sorting exercise.

Here's the truth: **the line between needs and wants isn't always clear.** And that's okay. Personal finance is *personal.* What's a need for one person might be a want for another. What matters is that you're *thinking* about it intentionally.

> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "Is internet a need or a want? Twenty years ago, it was obviously a want. Today? Try applying for a job without it. Try doing homework without it. The world changed, and the categories changed with it."

> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "And there's no shame in any answer. A gym membership might be a 'want' on paper, but if it's managing your blood pressure and keeping you off medication, it's doing the job of a 'need.' Context matters."

### Gray Area Discussions

**#8 — Home Internet: Need or Want?**
In the modern world, internet is increasingly essential for work, school, healthcare (telehealth), and banking. **Our take:** A basic internet plan is a need for most people. A premium high-speed plan with extra features leans toward want. If you work from home, it's definitely a need.

**#16 — Gym Membership: Need or Want?**
For most people, this is a want—you can exercise for free. BUT if your doctor prescribed exercise for a health condition, or the gym provides the only safe place to exercise in your area, it moves toward need. **Our take:** Default = want, but your situation may differ.

**#18 — Life Insurance: Need or Want?**
If you have dependents who rely on your income, life insurance is a need. If you're single with no dependents, it's more of a want/savings vehicle. **Our take:** Need if you have dependents; want/savings otherwise.

**#22 — Child's Extracurriculars: Need or Want?**
Technically a want. Emotionally? It can feel like a need. A child's social development, physical activity, and enrichment matter. **Our take:** Want, but a high-priority one that many families rightly choose to fund. No guilt either way.

**#27 — Basic Haircut: Need or Want?**
Professional grooming can be required for work. Going months without a haircut may not be realistic. **Our take:** Basic haircuts at reasonable intervals = need for most working adults. Frequent, premium styling = want.

**#29 — Pet Food: Need or Want?**
You made a commitment to your pet. They have to eat. **Our take:** If you have a pet, pet food is a need. (The decision to *get* a pet is a want, but once you have one, their basic care is a need.)

### Game Mechanics

#### Structure
- The month is divided into **4 weeks**
- Each week, the player receives a **crisis card** (random event)
- The player must decide how to respond using available options
- Decisions affect the **Financial Health Score (FHS)**: 0–100

#### Financial Health Score (FHS) Components
| Component | Max Points | Description |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| Bills Paid | 40 | All essential bills paid on time |
| Savings Protected | 20 | Emergency fund not depleted |
| No New Debt | 20 | Didn't borrow to cover expenses |
| Wants Preserved | 10 | Maintained some quality of life |
| Buffer Intact | 10 | Kept some buffer for future |

#### Scoring
| Grade | Score Range |
|-------|-----------|
| A | 90–100 |
| B+ | 85–89 |
| B | 80–84 |
| C | 70–79 |
| D | 60–69 |
| F | Below 60 |

---

### Difficulty Tiers

#### Tier 1: Breezy Month 🌤️
One minor crisis. Good for building confidence.

#### Tier 2: Stormy Month ⛈️
Two moderate crises. Requires trade-offs.

#### Tier 3: Hurricane Month 🌪️
Three or more crises, including at least one major. Tests everything.

---

### Crisis Draw Rules

Each difficulty tier determines how many and which type of crisis cards are drawn:

- **Tier 1 (Breezy):** Draw **1 card** from the Minor pool (Crises 1–4). One crisis occurs in a random week (Weeks 1–3).
- **Tier 2 (Stormy):** Draw **1 card** from the Minor pool + **1 card** from the Moderate pool (Crises 5–8). Assign to two different weeks (Weeks 1–3), randomly ordered.
- **Tier 3 (Hurricane):** Draw **1 Minor** + **1 Moderate** + **1 Major** (Crises 9–12). Assign to Weeks 1, 2, and 3 respectively. Week 4 may include a second Minor draw if the player's FHS is still above 70 (the game pushes back against comfortable runs).

Cards are drawn without replacement within a single playthrough. On replay, the pool resets.

### Decision Framework (Taught During Gameplay)

When a crisis hits, use this priority order:

1. **Can I cover it from my buffer?** (Best option—this is what it's for)
2. **Can I reduce wants to cover it?** (Second best—temporary sacrifice)
3. **Can I negotiate a payment plan?** (Third—spreads the impact)
4. **Should I use emergency savings?** (Fourth—only for true emergencies)
5. **Can I increase income temporarily?** (Fifth—side gig, sell something)
6. **Credit card as last resort** (Worst—creates new debt)

### React Implementation Spec

#### State Machine

The game follows a linear state machine with branching decisions at each crisis point:

```
SETUP → WEEK_1_BILLS → [CRISIS_1?] → DECISION_1 → WEEK_1_RESOLVE
      → WEEK_2_BILLS → [CRISIS_2?] → DECISION_2 → WEEK_2_RESOLVE
      → WEEK_3_BILLS → [CRISIS_3?] → DECISION_3 → WEEK_3_RESOLVE
      → WEEK_4_BILLS → [CRISIS_4?] → DECISION_4 → WEEK_4_RESOLVE
      → GAME_SUMMARY → [REPLAY_PROMPT]
```

**States:**
| State | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| `SETUP` | Player selects Chapter track (7 or 13) and difficulty tier. Initial state is loaded. |
| `WEEK_N_BILLS` | Display bills due this week. Player confirms payment. Balances update. |
| `CRISIS_N` | If a crisis is drawn for this week, display the crisis card with options. Skipped if no crisis this week. |
| `DECISION_N` | Player selects a response option. FHS and balances update accordingly. |
| `WEEK_N_RESOLVE` | Show updated budget snapshot: remaining balances per category, FHS, and a brief narrative consequence. |
| `GAME_SUMMARY` | Final FHS calculated. Grade assigned. Debrief questions displayed. |
| `REPLAY_PROMPT` | Offer replay options (see Replay Mechanic below). |

#### Crisis Draw & Event System

```typescript
interface CrisisCard {
  id: number;                    // 1-12
  tier: 'minor' | 'moderate' | 'major';
  title: string;
  description: string;
  cost: number;                  // Dollar impact
  options: CrisisOption[];
}

interface CrisisOption {
  label: string;
  description: string;
  fhsImpact: number;            // Negative number (penalty)
  balanceEffects: BalanceEffect[]; // Which categories are debited/credited
}

interface GameState {
  track: 'ch7' | 'ch13';
  tier: 1 | 2 | 3;
  week: 1 | 2 | 3 | 4;
  fhs: number;
  balances: Record<string, number>; // Category → remaining $
  savings: number;
  crisisHistory: { crisisId: number; chosenOption: number }[];
  drawnCrises: CrisisCard[];     // Drawn at game start based on tier
}
```

**Draw algorithm:**
1. At `SETUP`, based on selected tier, draw cards per the Crisis Draw Rules (see above).
2. Shuffle drawn cards and assign to weeks.
3. Store drawn crisis IDs and assignment in `GameState.drawnCrises`.
4. Use a seeded random (seed displayed to player) so the same seed reproduces the same crisis sequence for fair comparison on replay.

#### Replay Mechanic

After `GAME_SUMMARY`, the player is offered three replay modes:

1. **Same Crises, Different Choices:** Replay with the identical crisis sequence (same seed) but make different decisions. The summary screen shows a side-by-side comparison of scores and choices between the original run and the replay.
2. **New Random Crises:** Reshuffle the crisis pool with a new seed. Play a fresh month at the same difficulty tier.
3. **Change Difficulty:** Select a different tier and play with a new draw.

The replay comparison view highlights which decisions led to the biggest FHS differences, reinforcing the lesson that *choices* matter more than *circumstances*.
