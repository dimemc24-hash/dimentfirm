### What Is a Discharge?

Let's start with the key term:

> **Discharge** — A court order that releases you from personal liability for certain debts. Once a debt is discharged, the creditor (the person or company you owed money to) can no longer try to collect it from you. They can't call you, send you bills, sue you, or garnish your wages for that debt.

Your discharge is not a suggestion. It's a **federal court order.** It has the full weight of the law behind it.

> 🐇🐢 **Both say:** "Your discharge paper might not look dramatic — it's usually just a single page. But legally? It's one of the most powerful documents you'll ever receive."

---

### Chapter 7: Your Clean Slate

:::chapter-7
> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "Okay, Chapter 7 friends — let's talk about what just happened for you."
:::

**How Chapter 7 works (the short version):**

Chapter 7 is sometimes called "liquidation bankruptcy." Here's what that means in plain language:

1. **You filed a petition** telling the court you couldn't pay your debts
2. **A trustee** (a person appointed by the court) reviewed your assets
3. **Exempt property** (things the law says you get to keep, like basic household items, often your car, sometimes your home) was protected
4. **Non-exempt property** (if any) may have been sold to pay creditors
5. **Qualifying debts were discharged** — meaning legally erased

**What debts were typically discharged:**
- Credit card balances
- Medical bills
- Personal loans
- Utility bills that were past due
- Some older tax debts
- Deficiency balances (the amount left after a repossession or foreclosure sale)

**What debts usually survive Chapter 7** (these are NOT discharged):
- Most student loans (unless you filed a separate action called an "adversary proceeding" and won)
- Child support and alimony
- Recent tax debts (generally from the last 3 years)
- Debts from fraud or intentional harm
- Court-ordered fines and restitution
- Any debts you agreed to keep paying (called "reaffirmed" debts)

> **Reaffirm** — To sign a new agreement during bankruptcy to keep paying a specific debt, usually to keep the property attached to it (like a car loan). If you reaffirmed a debt, that one still exists.

**The timeline for Chapter 7:**
- The process typically took **3–6 months** from filing to discharge
- The bankruptcy **stays on your credit report for 10 years** from the filing date
- However — and this is important — **its impact on your credit score decreases every year,** especially as you build positive credit history

:::chapter-7
> 🐇 **Hariette says:** "Ten years sounds scary, I know. But here's what nobody tells you: most Chapter 7 filers see real credit score improvement within 12–18 months. The record is there, but what matters more is what you do NEXT. And that's exactly what this course is about."
:::

**What your clean slate means practically:**
- Those debts are gone. You do not owe them.
- If a creditor contacts you about a discharged debt, **they are breaking the law.** You can report them.
- You can start rebuilding credit immediately
- You are eligible for certain loans sooner than you might think (FHA mortgage: 2 years post-discharge; conventional mortgage: 4 years)

### Chapter 13: Your Marathon, Completed

:::chapter-13
> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "Chapter 13 crew — let's talk about what you accomplished."
:::

**How Chapter 13 works (the short version):**

Chapter 13 is called a "wage earner's plan." Here's what happened:

1. **You proposed a repayment plan** to the court — a detailed plan for paying back some or all of your debts over 3–5 years
2. **The court approved the plan** and appointed a trustee to oversee it
3. **Every month for 3–5 years,** you made payments to the trustee, who distributed the money to your creditors
4. **You completed the plan.** Every required payment, made.
5. **Remaining qualifying debts were discharged**

Let's be very clear about what this means: **you made 36 to 60 monthly payments, on time, while managing all your regular living expenses.** That is an extraordinary demonstration of financial discipline.

**What debts were handled through your plan:**
- Mortgage arrears (back payments) — brought current through the plan
- Car loans — may have been paid in full or at reduced amounts
- Credit cards and medical bills — paid a percentage based on your plan
- Tax debts — often paid in full through the plan
- Priority debts — paid in full

**What may have been discharged after plan completion:**
- Remaining balances on unsecured debts (credit cards, medical bills) that weren't fully paid through the plan
- Some debts that can't be discharged in Chapter 7 may be dischargeable in Chapter 13 (this varies — check with your attorney if you're unsure)

**The timeline for Chapter 13:**
- Your case lasted **3–5 years**
- The bankruptcy **stays on your credit report for 7 years from the filing date** (not from the discharge date — the filing date, which means several years have already passed)
- Since you filed 3–5 years ago, you may only have **2–4 years remaining** on your credit report

:::chapter-13
> 🐢 **Sheldon says:** "Did you catch that? Because you already spent years in your plan, you might only have a couple of years left before the bankruptcy falls off your report entirely. That marathon you ran? It's already paying off in ways you might not have realized."
:::

**What your completed plan means practically:**
- Discharged debts are gone — same legal protection as Chapter 7
- You likely kept major assets (home, car) that might have been lost in Chapter 7
- You've already demonstrated to future lenders that you can make consistent payments — that matters
- You have a track record of financial discipline that most people don't have
- You may be closer to major milestones (like mortgage eligibility) than Chapter 7 filers because more time has passed since your filing date

### What Both Chapters Share

No matter which chapter you filed:

**1. Creditor violations are illegal**
If any creditor contacts you about a discharged debt — by phone, mail, email, or anything else — they are violating the discharge order. Document it and consider consulting an attorney. You may be entitled to damages.

**2. Your discharge cannot be taken back** (in almost all cases)
A discharge is extremely difficult to revoke. The only way it can happen is if you committed fraud during the bankruptcy process. If you filed honestly, your discharge is permanent.

**3. You have a legal right to rebuild**
No one can deny you the right to open bank accounts, apply for credit, or participate in the financial system. Bankruptcy is a legal process, and your discharge is a legal resolution.

**4. You are under no obligation to explain your bankruptcy to anyone**
Your employer (current employer) generally cannot fire you for filing bankruptcy. Landlords and future employers may see it on a background check, and we'll discuss strategies for handling that in later modules.

| "Filing bankruptcy means I'm bad with money" | It means you used a legal tool designed for people in difficult financial situations. Period. |
| "I'll never recover financially" | Studies show most bankruptcy filers report improved financial well-being within 18 months of discharge. |

> - The average credit score increase in the first year after discharge is **50–100 points** for people who actively rebuild

> - Within 5 years of discharge, many filers have credit scores **above the national average**

### The SMART Framework

SMART is an acronym (a word made from the first letters of other words) that helps you turn vague ideas into concrete goals:

| Letter | Stands For | What It Means | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------------|---------|
| **S** | Specific | Exactly what do you want to accomplish? | "Save money" → "Save $500 for an emergency fund" |
| **M** | Measurable | How will you know you've achieved it? | "A lot" → "$500 in a savings account" |
| **A** | Actionable | What specific steps will you take? | "By setting aside $40 from each biweekly paycheck" |
| **R** | Realistic | Is this achievable given your current situation? | $500 in 3 months on your income = doable |
| **T** | Time-bound | When will you achieve this by? | "By June 1, 2026" |

**A vague goal:** "I want to get better with money."
**A SMART goal:** "I will save $500 in a separate savings account by June 1, 2026, by automatically transferring $40 from each biweekly paycheck."

See the difference? The second one tells you exactly what to do, when to do it, and how to know when you've succeeded.

### Goal-Setting Tips

✅ **Start small.** A goal you actually achieve is worth more than an ambitious goal you abandon.

✅ **Write them down somewhere you'll see them.** Phone wallpaper, sticky note on the mirror, wherever works.

✅ **Tell someone.** Accountability increases follow-through. A friend, family member, or even just this course.

✅ **Plan for obstacles.** Ask yourself: "What could get in the way?" and "What will I do if that happens?"

✅ **Celebrate milestones.** Hit 50% of your savings goal? That deserves recognition.

❌ **Don't compare to others.** Your starting point is unique. Your pace is valid.

❌ **Don't aim for perfection.** Missing a month of saving doesn't mean failure. It means you adjust and continue.
